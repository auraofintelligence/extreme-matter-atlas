Crystal Lab

Stack it, shift it, give it a twist

Salt grains are tiny cubes. Most snowflakes grow six arms. Honeycomb carries a heavy load with walls thinner than paper. Under each one sits the same quiet idea: a lattice, which is just a repeating arrangement of points. On the bench below you can pick a pattern, stack it four layers high, slide it half a step, and twist it until something strange appears.

Patterns you already own

🧂

The cube in your shaker

Tip salt onto a dark plate and look closely: each grain is a tiny box with flat faces. Sodium and chlorine atoms take turns on a grid of little cubes, and the grid shows up at a size you can see with a magnifying glass.

❄️

Six arms on the windscreen

Water molecules settle into rings of six as they freeze, so most snowflakes branch in sixes. The shape of one ring, far too small to see, sets the shape of the whole flake on your sleeve.

🍯

The strong room made of wax

Bees build six-sided cells with walls thinner than a sheet of paper, and the comb still holds kilograms of honey. The pattern does the heavy lifting, not the material.

The pencil in your drawer holds the star of this page. Its lead is graphite: sheets of carbon arranged in a honeycomb pattern, stacked like pages, each sheet one atom thick. A million of those sheets piled up would stand about as tall as three pages of a paperback. Each stroke you make shears tiny stacks of them onto the paper.

The sketchpad

Four patterns sit on the bench: the square, the triangle, the honeycomb, and the kagome, a weave of triangles and six-sided holes borrowed from Japanese basketry. Choose one and set how far apart the points sit, then stack up to four layers. AA places each layer directly on top of the last. AB slides every second layer half a step across, the way graphite stacks in your pencil. Twist turns each new layer by a small angle, and that is where things get strange.

Drag the picture to spin the stack and lean it over; the Tilt slider does the same by numbers. The Twist slider wakes up when Stacking is set to Twist. Each layer wears its own colour, and deeper layers sit dimmer.

The flyscreen trick

Lay one flyscreen on top of another and turn the top one a little. Large, slow ripples drift across the mesh, patterns far bigger than any single wire. That ghost pattern is called a moire (say mwah-ray): the big pattern two fine patterns make when they overlap slightly out of step. Two kitchen sieves held up to a window do it too.

The same trick works at the scale of atoms, and there it does more than look good. Graphene is a single sheet of pencil graphite, carbon in a honeycomb one atom thick. In physics labs, two graphene sheets twisted to about 1.1 degrees, roughly the angle a clock's minute hand sweeps in eleven seconds, carry electric current with no measured resistance once they are chilled below about minus 271 degrees Celsius, colder than the space between the stars. That is a measured result, first reported in 2018 and repeated in labs since.

The twist builds a moire pattern into the pair of sheets, a repeating landscape hundreds of times wider than the gap between atoms. Electrons slow right down inside that landscape, and in the slow crowd they pair up and flow without loss. Why the pairing happens is still being worked out, which is part of why so many labs keep building these little twisted stacks.

Press the Magic angle button in the sketchpad and look for the broad bright patch in the middle of the circle: that is the moire, drawn in points. Nudge the twist up to five degrees and the patches shrink into a pinwheel. Two flyscreens, two sieves, two sheets of carbon: same trick.

What the sketchpad draws and what it leaves out

The sketchpad draws geometry and nothing else. It places points, joins near neighbours, and colours the layers. It does not weigh a single force or energy, so it cannot tell you whether a stack would hold together, conduct, insulate or fall apart; that depends on which atoms sit on the points and what their electrons do.

Real crystal prediction adds physics maths on top of the geometry. For each candidate arrangement, a computer works out how comfortably the electrons would settle in; arrangements that settle into low energy are the ones that could survive as real crystals. GNoME, a crystal-hunting program built at Google DeepMind, ran that maths across millions of candidate arrangements and reported about 380,000 that look stable enough to be worth making T1. Cooking one a day, and cooking is lab talk for making the real crystal in a furnace, would keep a kitchen busy for more than a thousand years. Each of those candidates began the way your sketch did: as a guessed arrangement of points.

So have a guess. Stack a kagome AB. Twist a square by nine degrees. Lean a four-layer triangle right over and look along the rows. Some of the shapes worth cooking may be ones nobody has drawn yet. The sketchpad will not tell you whether your shape holds together; it hands you the part a crystal starts with, the pattern.
