One chart, every ingredient

The steel in a kitchen sink, the lithium in a phone battery, the aluminium in a stubby can, the copper in the wire behind your wall: nearly everything you can point at is built from the 118 boxes below, and most of it from a couple of dozen of them. The chart

Two figures on it are worth holding on to. Carbon melts at 3,823 K, about 3,550 °C: a wood-fired pizza oven runs near 450 °C, so carbon needs roughly eight of those stacked before it gives in. And osmium is the heaviest stuff here that anyone has ever weighed. Fill a 600 mL drink bottle with it and you would be holding about 13.5 kg, more than a full slab of cans.

Colour the chart by share of the crust and one box burns brightest: oxygen, which is about 46 per cent of the weight of the ground under your feet, locked into rock rather than floating about as air.

Click or tap a box for its card. Arrow keys walk the chart, Enter opens a card, Escape closes it. On a narrow screen the chart slides sideways inside its own frame.

How to read it

The chart is a seating plan. Every element gets one box, and where that box sits tells you how the atom is put together.

The small number

The number in the corner counts the protons packed into the middle of the atom: one for hydrogen, six for carbon, seventy-nine for gold. That count is the whole identity of an element. Change it and you are holding something else entirely, which is why turning lead into gold was never going to work over a kitchen stove.

Reading across

Start top left and read like a page of text: left to right, then drop down. Each new row wraps one more layer of electrons around the middle, so atoms further down the chart are bigger and heavier than the ones above them. The top row holds only two boxes because the first layer has room for only two.

Reading down

Now read straight down a column instead. A column is a family: its members carry the same number of electrons out on the rim, so they behave alike. The far left column holds metals soft enough to cut with a butter knife. The far right column holds gases that mostly keep to themselves.

The two loose rows

The two rows sitting below the chart belong inside it, at the dashed gaps in rows six and seven. They are lifted out so the whole thing fits on a page. Slot them back where they belong and the chart would be close to twice as wide.

Units on the cards

  • K Kelvin, a temperature scale that starts at the coldest anything can get. Ice melts at 273 K and a kettle boils at 373 K. Each card gives the everyday Celsius figure alongside.
  • g/cm³ What one cubic centimetre weighs, about a sugar cube's worth. Water is 1, so anything under 1 is lighter than water.
  • u How heavy one atom is, measured against a carbon atom set at 12.
  • pm A picometre: a millionth of a millionth of a metre. Atoms measure a hundred or two of them across.
  • ppm Parts per million, which works out as grams in a tonne. Ten ppm means ten grams of it in a tonne of rock.
  • kJ/mol The energy needed to pull one electron away, counted over a standard batch of atoms rather than a single one.

    Limits

    Every figure on a card describes the element in its ordinary form, near room temperature, with gases measured at normal air pressure. Squeeze an element, chill it, or catch it in a different form, and the numbers move. Carbon is the clearest example: at normal air pressure it goes straight from solid to vapour instead of melting, so the melting figure listed for it comes from work done under pressure.

    A dashed est. tag beside a figure means it was worked out from theory rather than weighed at a bench. Those cluster among the heaviest boxes, where only a handful of atoms have ever existed and each one fell apart in a moment. Nobody has held a gram of hassium to put on a scale.

    Crustal shares vary between surveys, since the crust is not the same everywhere you drill. Read them as a good guide to the order of size rather than to the last digit. Sizes on the cards are covalent radii: how far the middle of an atom sits from the middle of a partner it is bonded to. Measure an atom another way and you get a different number for the same element.

    Records listed on a card are measured records held under ordinary conditions. Change the conditions and some of them change hands.
