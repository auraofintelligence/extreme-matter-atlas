Thought experiment

Vimana

The old Sanskrit epics tell of palaces that fly. Suppose an engineer took those stories the way they would take a client's wish list, and priced it against the periodic table. The craft stays a wish. The records it would have to beat are already on the shelf.

The stories

In the Ramayana, one of the two great Sanskrit epics, the hero rides home aboard the Pushpaka vimana: a palace that moves through the sky at its owner's wish, roomy enough for a crowd, gentle enough that nobody aboard feels the ride. The Mahabharata, the other great epic, carries sky chariots of its own. These poems have been told and retold for well over two thousand years; call it eighty to a hundred generations of voices, each one choosing to keep the flying palace in the story.

India is not alone. Persian tales carry flying carpets, Chinese stories tell of cloud chariots, and medieval Irish annals tell of ships seen sailing through the air. People in many places have looked up and imagined riding the sky, long before anything made of metal did.

Read them here as stories first: kept and polished because people loved them, not as flight logs. But engineers do play a game with any wish list: take it seriously for an afternoon and work out what it would cost. That game is the whole of this thought experiment.

The design brief

An engineer reading those poems the way they read a client brief would jot down four requirements, and each one turns into a physics question with a number attached.

01 Lift, with no wings and no rotor in sight energy density
02 Near-silence, even up close momentum
03 Turns and stops that spare the passengers momentum
04 A hull that does not seem to mind heat heat shedding
🎈

Lift without wings

Hovering is expensive. A small two-seat helicopter holds itself up on roughly 100 kilowatts, about fifty kitchen kettles boiling at once, and it pays that bill by throwing a gale of air at the ground. The brief asks for the same lift with nothing visibly thrown. Whatever the craft would push against, the kettle bill would still come due, so the first question is energy density: how much go is packed into each kilogram carried on board.

🤫

Silence

Aircraft noise is mostly the sound of air being shoved. Physics keeps one firm rule here: every push needs a push-back. Engines that push something other than air do exist. An ion engine, which throws charged atoms instead of air, flies on deep-space probes today and runs close to silent; its push is about the weight of a couple of sheets of paper resting on your palm. Quiet thrust is real. Strong quiet thrust is the gap.

↩️

Sharp turns

Aerobatic pilots handle about ten g for a few seconds, where one g is the pull you feel standing still. A palace that stopped mid-air or turned on the spot at speed would put hundreds of g through everyone aboard; at one hundred g a passenger would press into their seat with about the weight of three utes. The brief quietly asks for a cabin where momentum, the oomph a moving thing carries, gets settled without the passengers paying for it.

🔥

A hull that shrugs at heat

Anything fast in air gets hot. The skin of a returning space capsule cops temperatures hotter than lava. Today's answers are to let a sacrificial layer char away, or to tile the craft in heat-proof ceramics. The brief describes a hull that lands looking like a palace, so it would need to shed that heat continuously and show nothing for it.

The universal shelf

Here is the quietly wonderful part, and it is measured, not imagined. Starlight carries a barcode. Split any star's light with a prism and thin dark lines appear at exact colours; each element prints its own line pattern, as personal as a fingerprint. In 1868 astronomers found a pattern in sunlight that matched nothing known on the ground and named the element helium, after the Greek word for the Sun. It took another 27 years for anyone to find helium here on Earth. The Sun's shopping list was read from 150 million kilometres away, a trip a passenger jet would need about twenty years of flying to make, and it was correct.

Every star measured so far prints its barcode from the same set of elements. So the shelf is universal: an engineer near any star would be shopping from the same stock list of 118 kinds of atom, and the entire list fits on one classroom wall chart. No secret ingredients have turned up anywhere light has been read.

Which means any real edge would come from arrangement, not ingredients. Three arrangements already show what that edge looks like.

💎

Perfect crystals

A metal's weakness mostly lives in its flaws. Tiny flawless whiskers of iron measure many times stronger than ordinary iron from the same shelf. Same atoms, tidier stacking, very different material.

⚖️

Chosen isotopes

An isotope is the same element at a slightly different weight. Diamond grown from carbon of a single weight sheds heat noticeably better than natural diamond. The chemistry is identical; the sorting does the work.

🕸️

Metamaterial lattices

A metamaterial is a material whose trick is its shape, not its chemistry. Built as a fine lattice, an ordinary substance can steer sound or light in ways no plain block of the same stuff can.

The hypothetical cabinet

If the brief forced a shopping list, an engineer might write down four wishes. Every line below is a wish, not a stock item; the measurements around each wish are real.

Metallic hydrogen

Squeeze hydrogen hard enough and the prediction, first made in 1935, is that it would turn into a shiny metal. Labs press samples between two shaped diamonds, point to point; one group reports a reflective sample at pressures beyond the centre of the Earth, like parking two thousand utes on a space the size of a fingernail, held for moments. If it could be kept stable at everyday pressure, and no lab has shown that so far, predictions say it could carry current without loss and pack several times the energy of petrol into each kilogram.

Room-temperature superconductor T1 · measured limits

A superconductor carries electric current without losing any of it as heat. The warmest confirmed one at everyday pressure works near minus 140 degrees, colder than the coldest outdoor air ever recorded on Earth. Under crushing pressure, hydrogen-rich compounds are reported superconducting near minus 20 degrees, close to a kitchen freezer. A superconductor happy in a warm room at everyday pressure would let a craft's wiring and magnets run without waste; none is confirmed so far.

Orbit-grown single crystals T1 · measured

Labs aboard the space station grow crystals in free fall, and many come back more even than the same recipe grown on the ground; reports describe fewer flaws. Today's best space-grown pieces sit closer to a sugar grain than a brick. A hull grown as one flawless piece would need that process scaled up millions of times, which nobody has attempted.

Lattices thinner than soap film T1 · measured

Graphene is a sheet of carbon one atom thick; a stack of three hundred thousand sheets would be about as thick as a page of a book, and each sheet is far thinner than the wall of a soap bubble. Tiny flawless pieces measure stronger for their weight than any steel. A hull skin assembled layer by chosen layer would be a material with no catalogue entry yet.

The spec builder

Pick a hull, a power source and a lift idea. Under each pick sits the real record it would lean on, and the size of the gap between that record and the brief. Each record is measured today; the craft is still a wish.

Limits. The poems are poems; no physical vimana has been measured, recovered or catalogued. Every number here comes from a present-day record. The craft itself has no measurement behind it, only a wish. If a workshop somewhere ever does produce a silent, wingless, heat-shrugging palace, the shelf it shops from will be the same 118 elements on the classroom wall.
