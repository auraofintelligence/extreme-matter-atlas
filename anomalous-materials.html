Samples on the bench

Every so often a piece of metal turns up with a story attached: it fell out of the sky, it was picked off a beach, it came from a crash. A handful of those pieces have been through real laboratories, sometimes several of them, decades apart. Laid out below are nine of them, with what was claimed, who measured what and when, and the ordinary material each recipe turns out to match.

The fingerprint a laboratory reaches for first

Pick up a light camera body, a bike frame or a laptop shell and there is a fair chance you are holding magnesium alloy: chosen because it is light, and because it can be cast into shape cheaply. Magnesium also happens to be what several of the samples below are mostly made of, which is lucky, because magnesium carries an unusually clean fingerprint.

Magnesium atoms behave the same way chemically, but they do not all weigh the same. The different weights are called isotopes: the same element in a different weight. Magnesium comes in three, a light one, a middle one and a heavy one, and any lump of the metal is a blend of all three.

Here is the useful part. Wherever you scoop magnesium from on this planet, the blend comes out close to the same: about 79 atoms in a hundred are the light one, about 10 the middle, about 11 the heavy. Beach sand, North Atlantic seawater, a bag of Epsom salts, a river in the Amazon, the magnesium in your own muscles: same blend, within a whisker. Meteorites carry it too, because they were made from the same cloud of dust the planets were.

That is why the blend would matter on a strange sample. The three weights are cooked up in stars, in different amounts depending on the star, and our whole neighbourhood settled into one ratio when the solar system formed. Something grown around a different star, out of a cloud with a different history, would carry a different ratio, and there is no cheap way to fake one: shifting the blend by hand means sorting atoms by weight, which is slow, costly and leaves traces of its own. So the weights are the first reading a laboratory takes when an odd sample lands on the bench. It is quick, the reference numbers are known to several decimal places, and there would be little argument about what an answer meant.

The thin bars under each fat one show the gap from Earth's blend, stretched so that a tenth of a percentage point is visible. Their full width is one and a half percentage points either way, which is far wider than any of the careful readings need.

One catch keeps the readings interesting. Heat, evaporation and hard knocks do nudge the blend a little: boil off some magnesium and the light atoms leave first, so what is left sits slightly heavy. That drift runs along a known line, and every solar-system material measured so far sits on that line, including the layered fragment further down. A sample sitting a little off the reference numbers can still be plain Earth material. What would stand out is a sample sitting off the line altogether.

What came back, and the everyday thing with the same recipe

The last column is where most of the work lands. For nearly every sample that reached an instrument, there is a material already made by the tonne somewhere on Earth with the same list of ingredients in roughly the same proportions, and several of them are made within a few kilometres of where the sample turned up.

Ubatuba magnesium fragments

1957 as claimed, possibly the early 1930s · Ubatuba coast, São Paulo state, Brazil

On 14 September 1957 Ibrahim Sued's column in the Rio de Janeiro paper O Globo printed a letter from a reader whose signature could not be read. He wrote that he had been fishing near Ubatuba when a disc climbing above the sea broke apart, and that he had picked light grey metal off the beach. Three pieces reached the paper, then the physician Olavo Fontes, then a private research group, then a Stanford professor, then a modern bench in 2017. Nobody has identified the man who wrote the letter.

What was claimed

  • 1960 Printed in the APRO Bulletin in March, and the origin of the whole story: the metal is pure magnesium, purer than anything obtainable in 1957, and therefore not made on Earth.
  • 2021 on Said in interviews by Garry Nolan, the Stanford immunologist who has run several of these samples: one Ubatuba piece put through a fine-beam instrument has magnesium weights about 30 per cent off the ordinary blend, which would mean somebody engineered it. No data table, uncertainty or reference standard for that run has been published. The same speaker says a second piece from the same event, measured on the same instrument on the same day, came back ordinary.

What was measured

  • Nov 1957 The Brazilian government's Mineral Production Laboratory, arranged by Fontes: no trace elements detected, written up as 100 per cent magnesium. Later readers took that as the instrument running out of sensitivity rather than as real purity.
  • Sep 1958 Oak Ridge National Laboratory, in a report reproduced in full in the 2022 journal paper: 99.8 per cent magnesium, with iron, silicon and aluminium in the parts-per-million range.
  • 1969 Roy Craig, writing up the metal for the Condon Report, had it compared against triply sublimed magnesium supplied by Dow Chemical from Midland, Michigan, and found the fragment less pure than the commercial metal being made in 1957. The composition settled near 99.9 per cent magnesium with strontium at 500 parts per million, zinc 500, barium 160, manganese 35, chromium 32 and copper 3. Craig noted that strontium was not then a known commercial addition to magnesium.
  • Feb 1968 The National Office Laboratory of the Alcohol and Tobacco Division, by neutron activation: 99.9 per cent magnesium. Its single isotope figure was later traced to the weight of the wrong sample, and is now read as a failed run rather than an oddity.
  • 1997 Reported by Peter Sturrock in the Journal of Scientific Exploration in 2001: all three magnesium weights read at once, and the sample sat about as far from the reference as the shop-bought standard run beside it did, both of them a fraction of a percentage point out.
  • 2017, 2018 Two commercial laboratories on the same dissolved drop. Cerium Laboratories in Austin returned 79.31 / 10.10 / 10.58; ICP & ICP-MS Services in Cleveland returned 79.28 / 9.94 / 10.85. Same liquid, different answers, and both reports are printed in the 2022 paper.
  • 2022 The finding published by Powell, Swords, Rodeghier and Budinger in the Journal of Scientific Exploration, from high-resolution mass spectrometry: the magnesium weights fall inside terrestrial limits. The trace-element weights disagreed between the two laboratories, so the paper draws nothing from them.
  • Across all of it What survives is chemistry rather than weights: consistently about 99.88 per cent magnesium carrying several hundred parts per million of strontium, which is roughly a pinch of salt in a one kilogram bag of flour.
  • 2022 Chemists at two magnesium firms told the same investigators that strontium is not a by-product of making magnesium and would have to be put in deliberately, and that the recipe was not in use when the sample surfaced.

Open questions

The paperwork is broken at the start and sound after it. There is no named finder, no fixed location and no confirmed date, so the metal cannot be tied to anything anybody saw. A later search for a matching event turned up a bright object over Ubatuba in 1933 or 1934, with light material caught in a fishing net around the same time, plus an undisputed aircraft crash in the area in April 1957. What is left is a workshop puzzle rather than a sky one: who made very pure magnesium with strontium added on purpose at that date, and why. The 2022 team recommend separating the trace metals from the bulk magnesium before re-running, because the magnesium outnumbers the traces about a thousand to one and swamps the detector, rather like trying to pick out one voice in a full grandstand.

Where measurement could go from here is sketched in Frontiers; what magnesium and strontium genuinely do is in Claimed Elements.

Council Bluffs molten metal

17 December 1977 · Big Lake Park levee, Council Bluffs, Iowa

At about a quarter to eight one December evening, several separate groups of people in Council Bluffs reported a glowing red mass coming down near Big Lake Park, then a flash and flames a few metres high. Police and fire crews were on the levee within about fifteen minutes and found molten metal running down the bank, glowing red-orange, spread over about two square metres, near enough to half a table tennis table, and weighing somewhere between 16 and 25 kilograms, about a bag of cement. The assistant fire chief's on-the-night estimate of ten centimetres deep does not square with that weight: two square metres of iron at that depth would run to about a tonne and a half, so one of the two figures is an eyeball estimate that missed. Eleven witnesses were documented, among them an assistant fire chief who said the cooled metal could be neither broken nor bent.

What was claimed

  • On the night Told by witnesses and recorded in the case file: an unidentified object hovering overhead dropped the molten mass. Some described something round with red blinking lights.
  • After The reading that grew up around it in books and articles rather than in reports: the metal itself would turn out to be unusual.
  • 2022 A narrower claim, made in a paper in Progress in Aerospace Sciences: material like this could be discarded slag from an advanced vehicle, and instruments built for biology could characterise it properly.

What was measured

  • On the night A radiation check at the scene: not radioactive.
  • Dec 1977 Ames Laboratory at Iowa State University, in an analysis by R. S. Hansen and E. DeKalb: chiefly iron with under 1 per cent of other metals, mainly nickel and chromium, plus a slag foam and a white ash of calcium and magnesium.
  • 21 Dec 1977 The laboratory at Griffin Pipe Products, four days after the fall: carbon 0.70 per cent, manganese 0.56, silicon 0.52, the rest traces. That is ordinary carbon steel of a grade poured in commercial foundries, two of which were working in Council Bluffs at the time.
  • 2022 Nolan, Vallée, Jiang and Lemke, on a surviving fragment: titanium, iron and chromium weights consistent with terrestrial values. One iron figure came back slightly high, and the authors put it down to a known interference, iron pairing with hydrogen inside the instrument.
  • 2022 The paper's stated finding: the components are consistent with a terrestrial origin, with no sign of being engineered or designed.
  • 2022 The oddity the same paper flags is compositional: five sub-samples differ from each other a lot, with aluminium varying two-fold and iron ten-fold, which reads as a rough industrial process rather than a designed alloy.

Open questions

The front of the trail is unusually good and the back of it is thin. Material went from a guarded scene to two named laboratories inside four days, which is why this case, rather than a more famous one, is the one that reached a journal. The specific fragment tested in 2022, though, is documented only as coming from one author's own holdings, with the intervening decades undescribed. The 1977 and 2022 element pictures also disagree: the newer charts put aluminium and silicon above iron in most sub-samples, where the 1977 work called it chiefly iron, and nobody has reconciled the two. The instrument could not see carbon or oxygen, so the obvious plain explanation, scrap metal melted by an improvised thermite reaction, could not be tested. Nothing in the metal is unusual. What stays unexplained is arrival: no foundry, transport route or spill has been matched to it, and a prank would mean somebody moved a bag of cement's worth of molten steel onto a park levee at night, which nobody reported seeing.

  • Vallée, "Physical Analyses in Ten Cases of Unexplained Aerial Objects with Material Samples", Journal of Scientific Exploration 12(3), 1998, Case 9

Ten samples, one catalogue

Published 1998, cases spanning 1933 to about 1978 · Brazil, the United States, Sweden, Colombia, Mexico

In 1998 Jacques Vallée published a survey in the Journal of Scientific Exploration pulling together ten incidents in which physical material was claimed to go with something seen in the sky. The aim was narrower than the subject suggests: to test whether laboratory work on such leftovers is workable at all, and to see what the evidence trail actually looked like when each case was examined one at a time. The ten run from Ubatuba and Maury Island through Sweden, Colombia, Mexico and Iowa.

What was claimed

  • Popularly In books and articles rather than reports: these samples are recovered fragments of craft of unknown origin.
  • 1998 The author's own claim, made in the paper itself, is smaller: a physical phenomenon has been reported over a long period, it is said to leave material traces, and those traces can be studied with ordinary laboratory methods.

What was measured

  • Across the ten The material falls into two ordinary families: light metals that conduct well, meaning magnesium and aluminium, and slag-like industrial leftovers, meaning iron with chromium, manganese and silicon.
  • Across the ten No rare element turned up, and no unusual isotope weight is reported anywhere in the set.
  • Sweden, 1956 Tungsten carbide at 94.9 per cent with cobalt at 4.1, analysed through SAAB and associated Swedish, Danish and German laboratories. Good quality for the early 1950s, and a known manufactured product; the same material tips a masonry drill bit.
  • Bogotá Aluminium 93.7 per cent with phosphorus 4.8, an unexplained surface layer, and, oddly for aluminium, no fluoride and no water. The author writes that a hoax could not be ruled out.
  • Campinas, Maumee, Jopala About 90 per cent tin; a fibrous piece at 92 per cent magnesium; plain iron with a little silicon, manganese and chromium.
  • Council Bluffs The set's best entry for paperwork, and the one case the author says met all four of his conditions. Its metal turned out to be ordinary carbon steel.

Open questions

The author's own list of limits is the load-bearing part of the paper: inaccuracies in data gathering, missing dates and times, no detailed field investigation, and no unbroken trail from ground to laboratory. One case in ten clears that bar. Kevin Randle, writing in 2021, argues the reverse of the paper's intent, that including cases with known trail failures, Maury Island above all, weakens the set as a whole. The argument the paper actually makes is about method rather than evidence: that comparative work is worth doing even when the material turns out mundane, and that field protocol has to improve before any sample could carry weight.

Sources
  • Vallée, "Physical Analyses in Ten Cases of Unexplained Aerial Objects with Material Samples", Journal of Scientific Exploration 12(3), 1998, 359-375 · semanticscholar.org
  • Sturrock, "Composition Analysis of the Brazil Magnesium", Journal of Scientific Exploration 15(1), 2001, the companion analysis of Case 1
  • SAAB and associated Swedish, Danish and German laboratories, via Sven Schalin, for the Väddö composition
  • Ames Laboratory and Griffin Pipe Products Company reports, December 1977, for Case 9

Maury Island slag

21 June 1947 · Maury Island, Puget Sound, Washington state

A harbour patrolman said that on a June day in 1947, while working in Puget Sound, he watched six large doughnut-shaped craft overhead, one of which shed a shower of light white material and heavier dark slag-like pieces that damaged his boat. His supervisor said he went to the beach and collected samples. A magazine publisher wired money to a pilot to investigate, and the visit from a man in dark clothing that the patrolman described is where the men-in-black motif starts. The residue was matched to the smelter across the bay.

What was claimed

  • 1947 Told by the two men to the publisher and later to federal investigators: the fragments were debris shed by a damaged disc, and the government suppressed the evidence.

What was measured

  • 1947 Testing arranged in Chicago by the publisher Ray Palmer identified the material as slag from steel production.
  • 1947 Army and FBI investigators compared it against slag from the Tacoma smelter across the bay, working since 1890 and pouring slag into the sound for decades, and found the two alike. The federal analysis results were not released publicly, which is the one real documentary gap here.
  • 1998 The element list carried in Vallée's catalogue is a routine smelter signature: calcium, iron, zinc and titanium as the main constituents, with aluminium, manganese, copper, magnesium, silicon, nickel, lead, strontium and chromium behind them and traces of silver, tin and cadmium. Nothing rare, nothing pure, no unusual weights.
  • 1947 The pilot who was sent to investigate examined the white metal produced for him and found it ordinary, and inconsistent with the finder's own description of what fell.
  • FBI file Federal files record the finder saying that if questioned he would call it a hoax because he wanted no further trouble, and record the pair approaching publications hoping to build the story into a paying deal.

Open questions

There is no collection record at all: the fragments were said to be picked up loose from a beach next to a working smelter district. Two investigators died when their aircraft came down near Kelso while carrying material from the case, which fed suppression claims for decades and adds no measurement to the pile. Edward Ruppelt, who headed the US Air Force study, called it the dirtiest hoax in the field in his 1956 book, and prosecution was dropped. What no measurement can supply here is the walk from the beach to the laboratory door, because nobody recorded it.

  • FBI investigative file on Dahl and Crisman, 1947

The layered magnesium piece

Received 1996, origin claimed as 1947 · posted to a radio host in Nevada; claimed origin New Mexico

In 1996 an anonymous correspondent calling himself A Friend posted metal fragments to Art Bell, host of the late-night radio programme Coast to Coast AM, with letters saying his grandfather had been on a 1947 recovery team in New Mexico and had taken the pieces from the underside of a wedge-shaped craft. The best-known piece is about 44 millimetres by 32 and 6 thick, near enough to a matchbox lid. It passed through private hands, then a research group, then To The Stars, which sold it to the US Army in 2019 for 35,000 US dollars, about A$49,000 at 71.7 US cents to the Australian dollar, 28 August 2026. In 2022 the US All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office contracted Oak Ridge National Laboratory to work on it. Nothing about where the piece was before 1996 has been documented.

What was claimed

  • 2018 on Carried in To The Stars' own material notes, with the provenance conceded as unverifiable: a layered magnesium-zinc and bismuth structure with no manufacturing precedent, the bismuth layers a small fraction of a hair's width at one to four micrometres, and the magnesium-zinc layers about one to two hair widths at one hundred to two hundred micrometres.
  • 2018 on That the layer spacing would make it a waveguide, a channel that steers a wave the way a hose steers water, for terahertz light, which sits between radio and infrared. The claim is that it would steer waves far longer than the channel should allow, and it has been linked publicly to reducing a craft's effective mass.
  • 2018 on That a search of national laboratories, metallurgists and the published literature found no evidence anybody had made one.
  • Jun 2018 Hal Puthoff, addressing a conference in Las Vegas, said it is about as close to technology as anything he holds.

What was measured

  • 1996 Nicholas Reiter, commissioned by the reporter Linda Moulton Howe, reported the piece as terrestrial, and later pointed at a specific industrial route: in the Betterton-Kroll process, used to clean bismuth out of lead, molten magnesium floated on liquid lead pulls the bismuth out as a magnesium-bismuth compound, and what it leaves behind is intergrown bismuth, lead and magnesium.
  • 2023 to 2024 Oak Ridge National Laboratory, on the parent sample and three earlier sub-samples: about 97.5 per cent magnesium by weight with zinc varying band to band between about 1 and 4 per cent, and columnar magnesium grains running across the banding.
  • 2024 Traces in parts per million: bismuth 3,813, lead 2,859, thallium 215, iron 55, gold 11. The bismuth is about 3.8 grams in a kilo, roughly a level teaspoon of sugar in a kilo bag of flour, and lead runs alongside it at close to one to one right through the bands.
  • 2024 Electron diffraction: the bismuth-rich bands are tiny disordered crystals in a glassy surround. The single clean bismuth crystal that the waveguide idea needs is not present, and the report adds that bismuth mixed with lead would not hold the electrical properties the idea rests on either.
  • Why lead Bismuth is the awkward element to test with, because it comes in one weight only: every bismuth atom on Earth is bismuth-209, so there is no ratio to compare. That is why the laboratory turned to the lead sitting beside it.
  • 2024 Magnesium weights: shifted by heat and strain, and sitting on the same line as solar-system materials generally, alongside meteorites, seawater, Earth rock and two brands of commercial magnesium metal.
  • 2024 Lead weights: inside the ordinary terrestrial field, and clear of the fields for lunar glass, Mars and meteorite minerals. Lead is the fingerprint geochemists reach for when they want to know where something came from, because three of its four stable weights sit at the end of a decay chain and ore bodies of different ages carry different mixes.
  • Apr 2024 The reported reading: made on Earth, using a mixture of elements uncommon by today's standards, then damaged by heat and force. The commissioning office set it beside mid-century magnesium alloy programmes, where zinc at 1 to 4 per cent and lead and bismuth additions for corrosion resistance were being tried.

Open questions

With no trail before 1996, no measurement can bind the piece to 1947 or to anything seen in the sky. The laboratories could not say whether it is a fragment of something larger, and noted that its layers have come apart and oxidised, so its present shape is probably not its original one, which leaves a waveguide argument about the original state not fully closed by structure alone. What was released is a laboratory synopsis and a supplement rather than a journal paper, and no independent laboratory has published a repeat of the magnesium or lead runs. The group that supplied it accepted publication and said the reports do not settle origin or purpose; its follow-up questions have not appeared. The researcher who rates the piece highest has not published isotope numbers of his own for it.

A claim about what a shape would do, arriving with a story wrapped around it: Vimana reads a much older set of stories the same way.

Flint Ridge aluminium

Mid-1990s as claimed, reported January 2026 · private land near Flint Ridge, central Ohio

A property owner in central Ohio said he saw a large unidentified object overhead in or around 1995, and afterwards found metal on his land. What eventually reached the assessing office was three bags of drill shavings, drilled by somebody else before the office took possession, plus one small sectioned piece. Oak Ridge National Laboratory ran the work and the report came out in January 2026, the second of two such reports on privately held metal.

What was claimed

  • Owner's account The specimen has unusual composition and structure, and it emits a gamma-ray signal. The claim is anchored to the owner's account of the object rather than to any documented recovery.

What was measured

  • Jan 2026 Bulk composition by weight, from Oak Ridge: aluminium 86.1 per cent, silicon 11.9, then iron 0.97, copper 0.37, magnesium 0.30, zinc 0.20, manganese 0.20, titanium 0.03. That is a conventional aluminium-silicon casting alloy, the family used for cast housings and engine parts, closest to two catalogue reference grades.
  • Jan 2026 Structure: silicon plates and needles in an aluminium surround, ordinary hard specks where iron and manganese have locked into the aluminium, small lead-rich pockets of the kind recycled metal leaves behind, and shrinkage holes up to about a millimetre, roughly the thickness of a five cent coin.
  • Jan 2026 Feature spacing of a few hundred micrometres, about the thickness of a business card, means the metal cooled slowly in a mould. A fast quench would have left features ten times finer, and it did not.
  • Jan 2026 Shavings and bulk interior matched chemically within instrument uncertainty, so the alloy identity is not an artefact of the drilling.
  • Jan 2026 About two days of gamma counting on three pieces: spectra indistinguishable from background. No radioactivity.
  • Jan 2026 The reported reading: most likely an ordinary, conventionally manufactured aluminium alloy. The supplement adds that the piece lacks the strontium or sodium modifiers a high-performance casting would carry, that its shape shows no working geometry, and that this alloy family has accounted for the bulk of shaped aluminium castings since the 1970s.

Open questions

No specific source, manufacturer or heat treatment could be pinned down; more pieces or factory records would be needed to narrow it to a catalogue grade. The owner's account is not independently corroborated, and nothing measured connects the metal to it. Isotope weights were not measured on this one at all, only elements, structure and gamma counting, so the test the rest of the collection leans on was not applied here. Aluminium would blunt it anyway: every aluminium atom on Earth is aluminium-27, so there is no ratio to compare, which is why the useful aluminium test is the cosmic-ray clock further down rather than the ordinary one. The shavings were drilled before the office took possession, so that handling is undocumented. The suggested plain candidates, a commercial casting, an industrial by-product, recycled alloy or a slow-cooling melt from a vehicle fire, are candidates rather than a match.

Uinta Basin livestock samples

1996 to 2004 · Uinta Basin, Utah, and wider Rocky Mountain states

The National Institute for Discovery Science, set up and paid for by the property developer Robert Bigelow in 1995, bought a 512 acre Utah ranch in 1996, about 207 hectares or near enough to 115 footy ovals, and put staff on it with cameras, sensors and a permanent presence. Alongside the ranch watch it ran a regional study of unexplained livestock deaths, led by the biochemist Colm Kelleher with veterinary help. Investigators went out with vets, carried out field examinations, and sent blood, tissue and eye fluid to laboratories. Most of the analysed cases were elsewhere in the basin rather than on the ranch itself, which matters when the results get quoted as ranch evidence. The institute wound down in 2004.

What was claimed

  • 1996 to 2005 In the group's own case write-ups and in the 2005 trade book by Kelleher and the reporter George Knapp: precise cuts made with a sharp instrument rather than by scavengers, no blood at the scene, no predator tracks, and in some cases warmth at the cut margins.
  • Jul 2001 In a technical report on a Dupuyer, Montana case: oxindole, a compound with a sedative effect, present in tissue and eye fluid of one affected animal and absent from a control animal.
  • 1996 to 2004 In the same body of reports: potassium chloride in blood at about ten times the normal level, copper shortfalls in six documented cases, and phenolic compounds with trace formaldehyde in some specimens.
  • 1998 A blue gel-like substance recovered from a site, said to have one rough precedent from the 1970s and none locally.

What was measured

  • 1996 to 2004 Real bench work sits behind this one: the chemicals were drawn out of the tissue, then split apart and weighed piece by piece on instruments that identify what a sample is made of, run against control animals, and written up in about twenty technical reports.
  • Where they went Those reports were published by the body that funded the work and none went to a journal. The individual laboratory reports behind them have not been released as documents, and no single archive of observation logs, calibration records or negative results has been released for anyone outside to work through.
  • 1 Jan 2001 The Deseret News reported that two university veterinary laboratories had confirmed that an eye and part of an ear in a 1998 case were removed with a sharp instrument rather than by scavengers. The universities are not named in the reporting.
  • Jul 1999 The Deseret News reported the 1998 blue gel as still being analysed, with results due soon. No identification has been published in the years since. Formaldehyde, which later summaries mention, is a common preserving fluid, and that is the first thing a written report would need to address.
  • Later Kelleher, the group's deputy administrator and the man who led the animal work, wrote that years of focused investigation produced very little physical evidence, and none that could be considered conclusive proof of anything.

Open questions

Which two university laboratories confirmed the sharp-instrument finding, and are their reports retrievable? How many of the analysed animals were actually on the ranch as against elsewhere in the basin? Where did the tissue and blood from the best-known 1997 case go, and does the internal report from that year hold analytical results or only field observations? It is not publicly documented who holds the group's archive now. A later government contract, the Defense Intelligence Agency's Advanced Aerospace Weapon System Applications Program, ran on the same property from 2008; the official historical record published in March 2024 confirms the activity took place and was not requested in the contract's statement of work, and its published output is literature reviews rather than sample reports.

Skinwalker Ranch mesa material

2020 to 2026 · a ranch mesa in the Uinta Basin, Utah

The Secret of Skinwalker Ranch premiered on the History channel in March 2020, filming on the same Utah property the earlier group had watched. The owner is the property developer Brandon Fugal; the principal investigator is Erik Bard, a physicist with patents in X-ray instrumentation; the astrophysicist and aerospace engineer Travis Taylor joined from the second season. Since 2020 the team has drilled and dug into a mesa on the property and sifted the spoils with a magnetic wand. Ceramic-looking shards came up from around 143 to 152 metres down, deeper than the Sydney Harbour Bridge is tall, and small metal flakes from around 10 and 16 metres. Both were carried to instruments on camera. Seven seasons in, the material claims arrive with named instruments and named people, and no written laboratory report with sample numbers, methods, detection limits and control samples has been published for any of them.

What was claimed

  • 2022, season 3 Material from horizontal drilling into the mesa read on a hand-held X-ray analyser as an alloy of iron, silicon, aluminium, magnesium, calcium and manganese. The instrument was a SciAps X-550, brought by the company's chief technology officer Dave Day and used by Tom Lewis, Bryant Arnold and Erik Bard. SciAps makes analysers rather than running a testing laboratory, and published the visit on its own company blog.
  • Aug 2025, season 6 The one instance with a named academic laboratory. Ceramic fragments from the drilling spoils were examined by Brian Patchett at Utah Valley University on a Tescan scanning electron microscope with an EDAX X-ray attachment, at 20 kilovolts. On camera, rectangular features and holes appeared to grow while the beam scanned, and to be gone when the microscope was switched off and on again. It was presented as the ceramic healing itself, and the operator is quoted saying he has not seen material do that. No written report from the university has been published.
  • 2025 A run on ceramic material, burning a speck and reading the colours of light it gives off, was reported as titanium, calcium, iron, aluminium and vanadium, described on air as similar to the ingredients of stealth materials.
  • 9 Jul 2026 In "Rocket Men", season 7, Erik Bard read a fresh flake on his portable analyser as iron and aluminium with no europium and no tellurium, presented as a difference from earlier flakes reported with both. Those two were described on air as elements found in the materials used to make spacecraft.
  • Ongoing The property owner has said the material is manufactured, is not a natural part of the local soil, and that this has been verified. No laboratory is named and no document is pointed to.

What was measured

  • The 2022 list Iron with silicon, aluminium, magnesium, calcium and manganese is a foundry list: it is the recipe of ferrosilicon and of the inoculant and deoxidiser alloys stirred into molten iron. It is also close to the bulk chemistry of the local chalky mudstone with rig steel mixed through it.
  • The ceramic The X-ray composition behind it, which was not shown in full on air, ran to roughly 1 per cent iron, which fits alumina or zirconia, the two hard ceramics used for wear parts. Those two are the standard materials for mud-pump liners on a drilling rig, which is the machine that made the spoils the shards came out of. A liner grinds itself away in service, and the pieces it sheds go out with the returns.
  • The effect Arnold Kruize, a scanning electron microscope specialist who worked for the microscope's maker, attributes the growing and vanishing features to charging: an uncoated insulating ceramic collects electrons under the beam and lets them go again, and that looks like holes opening and closing. The standard fixes, a conductive coating or a low-vacuum mode, were not used on screen, and the imaging voltage changed between shots.
  • Ti, Al, V Titanium with aluminium and vanadium is the fingerprint of Ti-6Al-4V, roughly 6 per cent aluminium and 4 per cent vanadium, the most-used titanium alloy on Earth and about half the aerospace titanium market. Vanadium is also close to the local default: the ranch sits on the Green River Formation, an organic-rich mudstone that concentrates vanadium, nickel, copper, cobalt and zinc, and the gilsonite mined in veins across the same county carries vanadium as a matter of course.
  • Eu and Te Europium is a phosphor: red and blue emitters in lamps, displays and banknote security ink, and control rods that soak up neutrons. Tellurium goes into thermoelectric modules, thin-film solar cells, rewritable discs, and steel and copper as a machining additive. Neither is a spacecraft structural material. Both also sit in a crowded part of the X-ray spectrum on a hand-held instrument, where their lines are easy to confuse with lines from commoner elements in an iron-rich sample.
  • The instrument A hand-held X-ray analyser returns a rough element list for a surface. It does not return crystal phase, structure, trace fingerprint or isotope weights, which is what an origin claim would rest on.
  • In writing Nothing has been released for any of it: no spectra, no concentrations with uncertainties, no detection limits, no blanks, and no drilling-mud control, which for material sifted out of a spoils pile is the first control a written report would need.
  • Next steps The team said the flakes were going to Utah Valley University for further work; no result has appeared, and the microscope operator has reportedly cited a non-disclosure agreement. The same university has received a five million US dollar donation from the ranch's owner. Both are on the public record.

Open questions

What is the local background level of europium and tellurium in mesa rock, and does any flake sit above it? Tellurium turns up in the water that comes up alongside local oil and gas, and the basin's rock carries a natural scatter of uranium and vanadium, so a run against plain mesa rock from the same depth, and against a shop-bought furnace-lining ceramic, would settle most of this in an afternoon. What is the trail from borehole to instrument, and who held each piece along it? Would the ceramic still appear to heal with a conductive coating on it and the voltage held steady, which is the one change that separates charging from a material doing something new? One more thing worth recording plainly: several websites state that a January 2026 paper in the Journal of Scientific Exploration published the ranch's 2022 to 2025 sensor data. The journal's own article index returns no such paper, and those pages give no volume, page numbers, author list or reference number. A separate document covering electromagnetic and radiation readings sits on ResearchGate, posted there by its author rather than published in a journal.

What europium, tellurium, titanium and vanadium genuinely do, and what each one would be good for testing, is in Claimed Elements.

No papers yet, in the owner's words

August 2025 · a public post

Asked directly by a follower why his team had not put its findings through peer review, the Utah ranch's owner answered at length in a public post. It is the clearest statement on the record about the state of the written output, from the person who commissions it. It is not a claim about a sample; it is a claim about the absence of paperwork.

What was claimed

  • 10 Aug 2025 Posted publicly by Brandon Fugal: the team has not yet submitted papers for peer review, for five stated reasons.
  • One The experiments are ongoing and the events are transient and unpredictable, while publication needs robust repeatable evidence.
  • Two Equipment failures, power loss and data corruption at key moments.
  • Three The work spreads across physics, biology, psychology and archaeology.
  • Four Phenomena that appear to actively evade detection, described as cat-and-mouse behaviour.
  • Five That submitting inconclusive findings early would damage credibility.

What was measured

  • Nothing on a bench No instrument was involved in this one. What can be checked is the statement itself, which is on the record and has not been withdrawn.
  • 2021 In earlier exchanges over a dropped-bottle experiment, the position given was that releasing partial data before review would confuse the issue. Those text files have not been released.
  • Ranch experiments Two university staff members who took part did so under a non-disclosure agreement, according to a news item published by their own university, the University of Alabama in Huntsville.

Open questions

Is any manuscript in preparation or under review, and with which journal? Whether the agreements around the current programme belong to the broadcaster or to the ranch is not publicly documented. The three collections that would answer most of the questions on the two Utah cards, the 1996 to 2004 archive, any analytical reports from the government contract that followed it, and the current team's raw instrument logs and physical samples, are all held privately, and none has been released.

Where the paperwork goes thin

The thread that breaks in most of these cases is not the laboratory work. It is the walk from the ground to the laboratory door: who picked the piece up, on what day, and who held it between then and the instrument. That gap sits on most of this bench, including the two most famous fragments, and it is the reason a clean chemical result still leaves the case open.

A single reading, unrepeated, is a starting point rather than a finish. The Ubatuba pair showed why: two laboratories given the same drop of dissolved metal returned different trace numbers, and that disagreement is what stopped the trace reading cold.

Ordinary is a common result here, and it is still a result. Several of these cases came back as plain steel, plain aluminium or plain smelter slag, and what stayed unexplained afterwards was how the material arrived, which is a different question from what it is made of.

Figures given on air or in an interview stand as they were given. Without the working beside them they cannot be followed in either direction, and a figure nobody has followed is not the same thing as a figure somebody has found wrong.

What would move the question

Read across nine cases and the shape of a decisive result comes into focus. Six things would do it, they can be done in any order, and not one of them needs an invention. Two of the six are ordinary weekly work in meteorite laboratories already.

A blend off the line

Not a tenth of a per cent off the reference numbers, which heat and handling can do on their own, but a magnesium blend sitting off the line that solar-system material follows. Something the size of a coin would be plenty.

The cosmic-ray clock

Out beyond the air and the Earth's magnetic shield, cosmic rays slowly build two rare atoms inside metal and rock: aluminium-26 and beryllium-10. Their ratio says how long an object sat exposed and how deep inside a larger body it rode. An accelerator mass spectrometer reads both from a gram of sample, and meteorite laboratories run it as routine. No sample above has publicly been through it.

Titanium's birthmark

Titanium comes in five weights, and the tiny differences between them split material that formed inside Jupiter's orbit from material that formed outside it. Grains older than the Sun sit hundreds of parts per thousand away from anything local. It is one of the sharpest origin tests going, it is ordinary work for a meteorite lab, and it has not been run on any of these samples.

Two labs that never spoke

The same material, split and sent to independent laboratories that do not compare notes and are not told what the sample is, each publishing its numbers, its standards, its blanks and its uncertainties.

A trail with names on it

A documented chain of custody: the paperwork that shows nobody swapped the sample. Who picked it up, when, who held it, who signed it across at every handover, from the ground to the instrument. This is the piece missing from nearly every case above, and it costs a notebook.

Rock from next door

For anything dug out of a mesa: soil and rock from off the property, from the same rock layer at the same depth, run on the same instruments in the same session as the sample. It is the cheapest gap on this list to close, and it turns an element list into a comparison.

The hopeful part is the instruments. A multicollector mass spectrometer, which weighs atoms one kind at a time and separates the three magnesium weights cleanly, is not exotic kit. Universities and geology labs around Australia and the world run them daily on rocks, seawater and meteorites, and the reference numbers they check against are already agreed. So the missing pieces are a notebook, an agreement between two labs, and a sample somebody is willing to cut in half. If those three ever line up, the reading itself would take an afternoon.

Nine samples have already given up their chemistry. The measurement still waiting is the one that would say where the atoms themselves were made, and the instruments that take it are running already, on meteorites, in laboratories here and overseas.
