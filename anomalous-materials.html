Samples on the bench

Every so often a piece of metal turns up with a story attached: it fell out of the sky, it was picked off a beach, it came from a crash. A handful of those pieces have been through real laboratories, sometimes several of them, sometimes decades apart. Laid out here are the ones with numbers behind them, each with what was claimed, what came back, and where the paperwork goes thin.

How to read this bench

Claims about a sample come from very different places, and the difference matters more than the wording does. Four tags do that job here. They sit on individual lines inside each case, so one case can carry several at once: a wild claim and a careful measurement often live in the same story.

  • T1Peer-reviewed measurement. A number that went past a journal's reviewers before it was printed, with the method written down beside it.
  • T2Named-lab report. A laboratory put its name to a written result, without a journal's review. Instruments and numbers are stated; nobody outside checked the working.
  • T3Broadcast or documentary claim. Said on air or in an interview. The figures may be right, but the spectra, error bars and blanks have not been published, so they cannot be checked either way.
  • T4Story or anecdote. An account from a person, a letter, a book or a newspaper column, with no measurement attached to it.
A tag is not a score. Plenty of T1 measurements came back ordinary, and a few T4 stories sit next to metal that is still puzzling chemists. The tag says where a line came from, and nothing more.

The fingerprint a laboratory reaches for first

Pick up a light camera body, a bike frame or a laptop shell and there is a fair chance you are holding magnesium alloy: chosen because it is light, and because it can be cast into shape cheaply. Magnesium also happens to be what several of the samples on this bench are mostly made of, which is lucky, because magnesium carries an unusually clean fingerprint.

Magnesium atoms behave the same way chemically, but they do not all weigh the same. The different weights are called isotopes: the same element in a different weight. Magnesium comes in three, a light one, a middle one and a heavy one, and any lump of the metal is a blend of all three.

Here is the useful part. Wherever you scoop magnesium from on this planet, the blend comes out close to the same: about 79 atoms in a hundred are the light one, about 10 the middle, about 11 the heavy. Beach sand, North Atlantic seawater, a bag of Epsom salts, a river in the Amazon, the magnesium in your own muscles: same blend, within a whisker. Meteorites carry it too, because they were made from the same cloud of dust the planets were.

That is why the blend would matter on a strange sample. The three weights are cooked up in stars, in different amounts depending on the star, and our whole neighbourhood settled into one ratio when the solar system formed. Something grown around a different star, out of a cloud with a different history, would carry a different ratio, and there is no cheap way to fake one: shifting the blend by hand means sorting atoms by weight, which is slow, costly and leaves traces of its own. So the weights are the first reading a laboratory takes when an odd sample lands on the bench. It is quick, the reference numbers are known to several decimal places, and there would be little argument about what an answer meant.

The thin bars under each fat one show the gap from Earth's blend, stretched so that a tenth of a percentage point is visible. Their full width is one and a half percentage points either way, which is far wider than any of the careful readings need.

One catch keeps the readings interesting. Heat, evaporation and hard knocks do nudge the blend a little: boil off some magnesium and the light atoms leave first, so what is left sits slightly heavy. That drift runs along a known line, and every solar-system material measured so far sits on that line, including the layered fragment further down this page. A sample sitting a little off the reference numbers can still be plain Earth material. What would stand out is a sample sitting off the line altogether.

The cases

Each card carries the same four parts: the story in brief, what was claimed, what the instruments returned, and the questions still open. The tag on the card title is the strongest kind of source behind the measurements, which is often weaker than the strongest source in the reading list underneath.

Ubatuba magnesium fragments

T1

1957 as claimed, possibly the early 1930s · Ubatuba coast, São Paulo state, Brazil

In September 1957 a Rio de Janeiro newspaper column printed a letter from a reader whose signature could not be read. He wrote that he had been fishing near Ubatuba when a disc climbing above the sea broke apart, and that he had picked light grey metal off the beach. Three pieces reached the paper, then a doctor, then a private research group, then a Stanford professor, then a modern bench in 2017. Nobody has identified the man who wrote the letter.

What was claimed

  • T4 The metal is pure magnesium, purer than anything obtainable in 1957, and therefore not made on Earth. This came from a 1960 bulletin and it is the origin of the whole story.
  • T3 Reported in interviews from 2021 onward: one Ubatuba piece run on a fine-beam instrument has magnesium weights about 30 per cent off the ordinary blend, which would imply somebody engineered it. Data tables, uncertainties and standards for that run have not been published.

What was measured

  • T2 1957, a Brazilian government laboratory: no trace elements detected, reported as 100 per cent magnesium. Later reviewers read that as the instrument running out of sensitivity, not as real purity.
  • T2 1958, Oak Ridge National Laboratory: 99.8 per cent magnesium, with iron, silicon and aluminium in the parts-per-million range.
  • T2 1961, Dow Chemical: 99.98 per cent magnesium, with strontium and barium near 30 parts per million.
  • T2 1968, a US federal laboratory: 99.9 per cent magnesium. Its single isotope figure was later traced to the weight of the wrong sample, and is now treated as a failed run rather than an oddity.
  • T1 1997, all three magnesium weights at once: the sample sat closer to the reference than two shop-bought standards sat to each other.
  • T2 2017 and 2018, two commercial laboratories on the same dissolved drop. Cleveland returned 79.28 / 9.94 / 10.85; Austin returned 79.31 / 10.10 / 10.58. Same liquid, different answers.
  • T1 The published finding, 2022: the magnesium weights fall inside terrestrial limits. The trace-element weights disagreed between the two labs, so no reading was taken from them.
  • T1 What survives is chemistry rather than weights: consistently about 99.88 per cent magnesium carrying several hundred parts per million of strontium, which is roughly a pinch of salt in a one kilogram bag of flour.
  • T2 Chemists at two firms told the investigators that strontium is not a by-product of making magnesium, and would have to be put in deliberately. That recipe was not in use when the sample surfaced.

Open questions

The paperwork is broken at the start and sound after it. There is no named finder, no fixed location and no confirmed date, so the metal cannot be tied to anything anybody saw. A later search for a matching event turned up a bright object over Ubatuba in 1933 or 1934, with light material caught in a fishing net around the same time, plus an undisputed aircraft crash in the area in April 1957. What is left is a workshop puzzle rather than a sky one: who made very pure magnesium with strontium added on purpose at that date, and why. The 2022 team recommend separating the trace metals from the bulk magnesium before re-running, because the magnesium outnumbers the traces about a thousand to one and swamps the detector, rather like trying to pick out one voice in a full grandstand.

Where measurement could go from here is sketched in Frontiers.

Council Bluffs molten metal

T1

17 December 1977 · Big Lake Park levee, Council Bluffs, Iowa

At about a quarter to eight one December evening, several separate groups of people in Council Bluffs reported a glowing red mass coming down near Big Lake Park, then a flash and flames a few metres high. Police and fire crews were on the levee within about fifteen minutes and found molten metal running down the bank, glowing red-orange, spread over roughly the footprint of a single bed and weighing somewhere between 16 and 25 kilograms, about a bag of cement. Eleven witnesses were documented, among them an assistant fire chief who said the cooled metal could be neither broken nor bent.

What was claimed

  • T4 An unidentified object hovering overhead dropped the molten mass. Some witnesses described something round with red blinking lights.
  • T4 The exotic reading that followed: the metal itself would turn out to be unusual.
  • T1 A narrower claim was made much later, in 2022: that material like this could be discarded slag from an advanced vehicle, and that instruments built for biology could characterise it properly.

What was measured

  • T2 On the night, a check at the scene: not radioactive.
  • T2 Within days, Ames Laboratory at Iowa State: chiefly iron with under 1 per cent of other metals, mainly nickel and chromium, plus a slag foam and a white ash of calcium and magnesium.
  • T2 Four days after the fall, a pipe foundry laboratory: carbon 0.70 per cent, manganese 0.56, silicon 0.52, the rest traces. That is ordinary carbon steel of a grade made in commercial foundries, two of which were working in Council Bluffs at the time.
  • T1 2022, a surviving fragment on modern instruments: titanium, iron and chromium weights consistent with terrestrial values. One iron figure came back slightly high, and the authors themselves put it down to a known interference in the instrument.
  • T1 The paper's stated finding: the components are consistent with a terrestrial origin, with no sign of being engineered or designed.
  • T1 The oddity the paper does flag is compositional: five sub-samples differ from each other a lot, with aluminium varying two-fold and iron ten-fold, which reads as a rough industrial process rather than a designed alloy.

Open questions

The front of the trail is unusually good and the back of it is thin. Material went from a guarded scene to two named laboratories inside four days, which is why this case, rather than a more famous one, is the one that reached a journal. The specific fragment tested in 2022, though, is documented only as coming from one author's own holdings, with the intervening decades undescribed. The 1977 and 2022 element pictures also disagree: the newer charts put aluminium and silicon above iron in most sub-samples, where the 1977 work called it chiefly iron, and nobody has reconciled the two. The instrument could not see carbon or oxygen, so the obvious plain explanation, scrap metal melted by an improvised thermite reaction, could not be tested. Nothing in the metal is unusual. What stays unexplained is arrival: no foundry, transport route or spill has been matched to it, and a prank would mean somebody moved a bag of cement's worth of molten steel onto a park levee at night, which has no evidence either.

Ten samples, one catalogue

T1

Published 1998, cases spanning 1933 to about 1978 · Brazil, the United States, Sweden, Colombia, Mexico

In 1998 Jacques Vallée published a survey pulling together ten incidents in which physical material was claimed to go with something seen in the sky. The aim was narrower than the subject suggests: to test whether laboratory work on such leftovers is workable at all, and to see what the evidence trail actually looked like when each case was examined one at a time. The ten run from Ubatuba and Maury Island through Sweden, Colombia, Mexico and Iowa.

What was claimed

  • T4 Popularly, that these samples are recovered fragments of craft of unknown origin.
  • T1 The author's own claim is smaller: that a physical phenomenon has been reported over a long period, that it is said to leave material traces, and that those traces can be studied with ordinary laboratory methods.

What was measured

  • T1 Across all ten, the material falls into two ordinary families: light metals that conduct well, meaning magnesium and aluminium, and slag-like industrial leftovers, meaning iron with chromium, manganese and silicon.
  • T1 No rare element turned up, and no unusual isotope weight is reported anywhere in the set.
  • T1 Sweden, 1956: tungsten carbide at 94.9 per cent with cobalt at 4.1. Good quality for the early 1950s, and a known manufactured product; the same material tips a masonry drill bit.
  • T1 Bogotá: aluminium 93.7 per cent with phosphorus 4.8, an unexplained surface layer, and, oddly for aluminium, no fluoride and no water. The author writes that a hoax could not be ruled out.
  • T1 Campinas about 90 per cent tin; Maumee a fibrous piece at 92 per cent magnesium; Jopala plain iron with a little silicon, manganese and chromium.
  • T1 The set's best entry for paperwork is Council Bluffs, the one case the author says met all four of his conditions, and its metal turned out to be ordinary carbon steel.

Open questions

The author's own list of limits is the load-bearing part of the paper: inaccuracies in data gathering, missing dates and times, no detailed field investigation, and no unbroken trail from ground to laboratory. One case in ten clears that bar. Critics argue the reverse of the paper's intent, that including cases with known trail failures, Maury Island above all, weakens the set as a whole. The argument the paper actually makes is about method rather than evidence: that comparative work is worth doing even when the material turns out mundane, and that field protocol has to improve before any sample could carry weight.

  • T1 Sturrock, "Composition Analysis of the Brazil Magnesium", Journal of Scientific Exploration 15(1), 2001, the companion analysis of Case 1

Maury Island slag

T1

21 June 1947 · Maury Island, Puget Sound, Washington state

A harbour patrolman said that on a June day in 1947, while working in Puget Sound, he watched six large doughnut-shaped craft overhead, one of which shed a shower of light white material and heavier dark slag-like pieces that damaged his boat. His supervisor said he went to the beach and collected samples. A magazine publisher wired money to a pilot to investigate, and the visit from a man in dark clothing that the patrolman described is where the men-in-black motif starts. This one is on the bench as a baseline: a fall story whose residue was matched to something local.

What was claimed

  • T4 The fragments were debris shed by a damaged disc, and the government suppressed the evidence.

What was measured

  • T2 Testing arranged in Chicago in 1947 identified the material as slag from steel production.
  • T2 Army and FBI investigators compared it against slag from a Tacoma smelter that had been working across the bay since 1912, and found the two alike. The federal analysis results were not released publicly, which is the one real documentary gap here.
  • T1 The element list carried in the later catalogue is a routine smelter signature: calcium, iron, zinc and titanium as the main constituents, with aluminium, manganese, copper, magnesium, silicon, nickel, lead, strontium and chromium behind them and traces of silver, tin and cadmium. Nothing rare, nothing pure, no unusual weights.
  • T4 The pilot who was sent to investigate examined the white metal produced for him and found it ordinary, and inconsistent with the finder's own description of what fell.
  • T2 Federal files record the finder saying that if questioned he would call it a hoax because he wanted no further trouble, and record the pair approaching publications hoping to build the story into a profitable deal.

Open questions

There is no collection record at all: the fragments were said to be picked up loose from a beach next to a working smelter district. Two investigators died when their aircraft came down near Kelso while carrying material from the case, which fed suppression claims for decades and adds no measurement to the pile. The head of the later official study called it the dirtiest hoax in the field, and prosecution was dropped. Its value on this bench is as a control: it shows what a case looks like when the residue does match the local industrial waste, which is a useful shape to have in your eye when reading the others.

  • T4 HistoryLink.org essay 2068, Dahl and Crisman report a 21 June 1947 explosion · historylink.org

The layered magnesium piece

T2

Received 1996, origin claimed as 1947 · posted to a radio host in Nevada; claimed origin New Mexico

In 1996 an anonymous correspondent posted metal fragments to a late-night radio host, with letters saying his grandfather had been on a 1947 recovery team in New Mexico and had taken the pieces from the underside of a wedge-shaped craft. The material passed through private hands, then a research group, then in 2019 into a co-operative agreement with a US Army research command. Nothing about where the piece was before 1996 has been documented. It is the sample most often described as the strongest one in private hands.

What was claimed

  • T3 A layered magnesium-zinc and bismuth structure with no manufacturing precedent, the bismuth layers thinner than a human hair and the magnesium layers about ten times a hair.
  • T3 That the layer spacing would make it a waveguide, a channel that steers a wave the way a hose steers water, for terahertz light, which sits between radio and infrared. The claim is that it would steer waves far longer than the channel should allow, and it has been linked publicly to reducing a craft's effective mass.
  • T3 That a search of national laboratories, metallurgists and the published literature found no evidence anybody had made one.
  • T3 A researcher who has examined it says it is about as close to technology as anything he holds.

What was measured

  • T2 2023 to 2024, Oak Ridge National Laboratory, on the parent sample and three earlier sub-samples: about 97.5 per cent magnesium by weight with about 2 per cent zinc, the zinc varying band to band.
  • T2 Traces in parts per million: bismuth 3813, lead 2859, thallium 215, iron 55, gold 11. The bismuth is about four grams in a kilo, roughly a level teaspoon of sugar in a kilo bag, and it sits alongside lead at about one to one right through the bands.
  • T2 Electron diffraction: the bismuth-rich bands are tiny disordered crystals in a glassy surround. The single clean bismuth crystal that the waveguide idea needs, according to the physics paper the idea rests on, was never present in the material.
  • T2 Magnesium weights: shifted by heat and strain, and sitting on the same line as solar-system materials generally, alongside meteorites, seawater, Earth rock and two brands of commercial magnesium metal.
  • T2 Lead weights: inside the ordinary terrestrial field, and clear of the fields for lunar glass, Mars and meteorite minerals.
  • T2 The reported reading: made on Earth, using a mixture of elements uncommon by today's standards, then damaged by heat and force. The commissioning office set it beside mid-century magnesium alloy programmes, where zinc at 1 to 4 per cent and lead and bismuth additions for corrosion resistance were being tried.

Open questions

With no trail before 1996, no measurement can bind the piece to 1947 or to anything seen in the sky. The laboratories could not say whether it is a fragment of something larger, and noted that its layers have come apart and oxidised, so its present shape is probably not its original one, which leaves a waveguide argument about the original state not fully closed by structure alone. The report is a laboratory synopsis rather than a peer-reviewed paper, and no independent laboratory has published a repeat of the magnesium or lead runs. The group that supplied it accepted publication and said the reports do not settle origin or purpose; its follow-up questions have not appeared. The researcher who rates the piece highest has not published isotope numbers of his own for it.

A claim about what a shape would do, arriving with a story wrapped around it: Vimana reads a much older set of stories the same way.

Flint Ridge aluminium

T2

Mid-1990s as claimed, reported January 2026 · private land near Flint Ridge, central Ohio

A property owner in central Ohio said he saw a large unidentified object overhead in or around 1995, and afterwards found metal on his land. What eventually reached the assessing office was three bags of drill shavings, drilled by somebody else before the office took possession, plus one small sectioned piece. Oak Ridge ran the work and the report came out in January 2026, the second of two such reports on privately held metal.

What was claimed

  • T4 The specimen has unusual composition and structure, and it emits a gamma-ray signal. The claim is anchored to the owner's account of the object rather than to any documented recovery.

What was measured

  • T2 Bulk composition by weight: aluminium 86.1 per cent, silicon 11.9, then iron 0.97, copper 0.37, magnesium 0.30, zinc 0.20, manganese 0.20, titanium 0.03. That is a conventional aluminium-silicon casting alloy, the family used for engine blocks and pump housings, closest to two catalogue reference grades.
  • T2 Structure: silicon plates and needles in an aluminium surround, ordinary iron and manganese intermetallics, small lead-rich pockets consistent with recycled feedstock, and shrinkage holes up to about a millimetre, roughly the thickness of a five cent coin.
  • T2 Feature spacing of a few hundred micrometres, about the thickness of a business card, means the metal cooled slowly in a mould. A fast quench would have left features ten times finer, and it did not.
  • T2 Shavings and bulk interior matched chemically within instrument uncertainty, so the alloy identity is not an artefact of the drilling.
  • T2 About two days of gamma counting on three pieces: spectra indistinguishable from background. No radioactivity.
  • T2 The reported reading: most likely an ordinary, conventionally manufactured aluminium alloy. The supplement adds that the piece lacks the strontium or sodium modifiers a high-performance casting would carry, that its shape shows no working geometry, and that this alloy family has accounted for the bulk of shaped aluminium castings since the 1970s.

Open questions

No specific source, manufacturer or heat treatment could be pinned down; more pieces or factory records would be needed to narrow it to a catalogue grade. The owner's account is not independently corroborated, and nothing measured connects the metal to it. Isotope weights were not measured on this one at all, only elements, structure and gamma counting, so the test the rest of this bench leans on was not applied here. The shavings were drilled before the office took possession, so that handling is undocumented. The suggested plain candidates, a commercial casting, an industrial by-product, recycled alloy or a slow-cooling melt from a vehicle fire, are candidates rather than a match.

  • T2 Fiscal Year 2024 consolidated annual report, recording the taskings and the concurrence with both findings · aaro.mil

Uinta Basin livestock samples

T2

1996 to 2004 · Uinta Basin, Utah, and wider Rocky Mountain states

A privately funded science group bought a Utah ranch in 1996 and put staff on it with cameras, sensors and a permanent presence. Alongside the ranch watch it ran a regional study of unexplained livestock deaths, led by a veterinarian and microbiologist. Investigators went out with vets, carried out field examinations, and sent blood, tissue and eye fluid to laboratories. Most of the analysed cases were elsewhere in the basin rather than on the ranch itself, which matters when the results get quoted as ranch evidence.

What was claimed

  • T3 Precise cuts made with a sharp instrument rather than by scavengers.
  • T2 Oxindole, a compound with a sedative effect, present in tissue and eye fluid of one affected animal and absent from a control animal.
  • T2 Potassium chloride in blood at about ten times the normal level, copper shortfalls in six documented cases, and phenolic compounds with trace formaldehyde in some specimens.
  • T3 A blue gel-like substance recovered from a 1998 site, said to have one rough precedent from the 1970s and none locally.

What was measured

  • T2 Real bench work sits behind this one: organic extractions, infrared spectrometry, and gas chromatography with mass spectrometry, run against control animals, written up in about twenty technical reports.
  • T2 Those reports were published by the body that funded the work and none went to a journal. The individual laboratory reports behind them have not been released as documents.
  • T3 Press reporting in 2001 said two university veterinary laboratories had confirmed that an eye and part of an ear in a 1998 case were removed with a sharp instrument rather than by scavengers. The universities are not named in the reporting.
  • T3 The 1998 blue gel was reported in 1999 as still being analysed, with results due soon. No identification has been published. Formaldehyde, which later summaries mention, is a common preserving fluid, and that is the first thing a written report would need to address.
  • T4 The group's own deputy administrator later wrote that years of focused investigation produced very little physical evidence, and none that could be considered conclusive proof of anything.

Open questions

Which two university laboratories confirmed the sharp-instrument finding, and are their reports retrievable? How many of the analysed animals were actually on the ranch as against elsewhere in the basin? Where did the tissue and blood from the best-known 1997 case go, and does the internal report from that year hold analytical results or only field observations? The group's observation logs, calibration records and negative results have not been released as one archive, and it is not publicly documented who holds them now. A later government contract ran on the same property between 2008 and 2012; the official record confirms the activity took place and was not requested in the contract's statement of work, and its published output is literature reviews rather than sample reports.

Mesa ceramic and metal flakes

T3

2022 to 2026 · a ranch mesa in the Uinta Basin, Utah

Since 2020 a television team has been drilling and digging into a mesa on the same Utah property, and sifting the spoils with a magnetic wand. Ceramic-looking shards came up from around 143 to 152 metres down, and small metal flakes from around 10 and 16 metres. Both were carried to instruments on camera, including a scanning electron microscope at a Utah university. This is the current programme's strongest material claim, and it is the one with the least written down.

What was claimed

  • T3 Under the electron beam the ceramic's surface pores appeared to enlarge, then appeared restored when the microscope was switched off and back on. The operator is quoted saying he has not seen material do that.
  • T3 Earlier flakes were reported on air as containing iron, aluminium, europium and tellurium, described as elements found in spacecraft materials.
  • T3 A hand-held X-ray reading on a 2022 mesa fragment was reported as an alloy of iron, silicon, aluminium, magnesium, calcium and manganese. The instrument maker published a note confirming which analyser was used and by whom.
  • T3 The property owner has said the material is manufactured, is not a natural part of the local soil, and that this has been verified. No laboratory is named and no document is pointed to.

What was measured

  • T3 In 2026 the team's own portable analyser read a fresh flake as iron and aluminium with no europium and no tellurium. The episode presented that as a difference from the earlier flakes.
  • T3 A 2025 run on the ceramic reported titanium, calcium, iron, aluminium and vanadium. Those five are ordinary constituents of both crustal rock and commercial ceramics.
  • T3 Nothing has been released in writing for any of it: no spectra, no concentrations with uncertainties, no detection limits, no blanks, and no drilling-mud controls, which for material sifted out of a spoils pile is the first control a written report would need.
  • T4 A microscopist with twenty-five years on these instruments attributes the ceramic effect to charging: an uncoated insulating ceramic collects electrons under the beam and discharges, which can look like holes opening and closing. The standard fixes, a conductive coating or a low-vacuum mode, were not used on screen, and the imaging voltage changed between shots.
  • T3 A hand-held X-ray analyser gives a rough surface element list. It does not give crystal phase, structure, trace fingerprint or isotope weights, which is what an origin claim would need.

Open questions

What is the local background level of europium and tellurium in mesa rock, and does any flake sit above it? The basin carries widespread weak uranium and vanadium mineralisation, and relatively high tellurium turns up in local produced water, so a comparison against plain mesa rock and against a commercial refractory ceramic would settle most of this quickly. What is the trail from borehole to instrument? Flakes and shards were said on air to be going to a Utah university; no result has been issued, and the microscope operator has reportedly cited a non-disclosure agreement. One more thing worth recording plainly: several web pages state that a January 2026 journal paper published the ranch's sensor data. The journal's own article index returns no such paper, and the pages give no volume, page numbers or author list.

No papers yet, in the owner's words

T4

August 2025 · a public post

Asked directly by a follower why his team had not put its findings through peer review, the Utah ranch's owner answered at length in a public post. It belongs on the bench because it is the clearest statement on the record about the state of the written output, from the person who commissions it. It is not a claim about a sample; it is a claim about the absence of paperwork.

What was claimed

  • T4 The team has not yet submitted papers for peer review, for five stated reasons.
  • T4 One: the experiments are ongoing and the events are transient and unpredictable, while publication needs robust repeatable evidence.
  • T4 Two: equipment failures, power loss and data corruption at key moments.
  • T4 Three: the work spreads across physics, biology, psychology and archaeology.
  • T4 Four: phenomena that appear to actively evade detection, described as cat-and-mouse behaviour.
  • T4 Five: that submitting inconclusive findings early would damage credibility.

What was measured

  • T4 Nothing went on a bench for this one. What can be checked is the statement itself, which is on the record and has not been withdrawn.
  • T4 In earlier exchanges over a 2021 dropped-bottle experiment, the position given was that releasing partial data before review would confuse the issue. Those text files have not been released.
  • T4 Two university physicists who took part in ranch experiments did so under a non-disclosure agreement, according to their own university's news item.

Open questions

Is any manuscript in preparation or under review, and with which journal? Whether the agreements around the current programme belong to the broadcaster or to the ranch is not publicly documented. The three collections that would answer most of the questions on the two Utah cards, the 1996 to 2004 archive, any analytical reports from the 2008 to 2012 contract, and the current team's raw instrument logs and physical samples, are all held privately, and none has been released.

Limits

Every number here comes from somebody else's bench, and the ones that matter most were often run once. A single laboratory's reading, unrepeated, is a starting point rather than a finish.

Where a case has no trail from the ground to the laboratory door, no measurement can tie the material to anything seen in the sky. That gap sits on most of this bench, including the two most famous fragments.

Ordinary is a common result here, and it is still a result. Several of these cases came back as plain steel, plain aluminium or plain smelter slag, and what stayed unexplained afterwards was how the material arrived, which is a different question from what it is made of.

Where figures are quoted from a broadcast or an interview, they are quoted as given. Without the working beside them they cannot be checked in either direction, and a figure that has not been checked is not the same thing as a figure that has been found wrong.

What would settle it

Read across nine cases and the shape of a decisive result comes into focus. Three things would have to arrive together, and none of them needs an invention.

A blend off the line

Not a tenth of a per cent off the reference numbers, which heat and handling can do on their own, but a magnesium blend sitting off the line that solar-system material follows. Something the size of a coin would be plenty.

Two labs that never spoke

The same material, split and sent to independent laboratories that do not compare notes, each publishing its numbers, its standards, its blanks and its uncertainties. The Ubatuba pair showed why: two labs on one drop of liquid returned different answers, and that disagreement is what stopped the trace-element reading cold.

A trail with names on it

A documented chain of custody: the paperwork trail that shows nobody swapped the sample. Who picked it up, when, who held it, who signed it across at every handover, from the ground to the instrument. This is the piece missing from nearly every case above, and it costs a notebook.

The hopeful part is the instruments. A multicollector mass spectrometer, which weighs atoms one kind at a time and separates the three magnesium weights cleanly, is not exotic kit. Universities and geology labs around Australia and the world run them daily on rocks, seawater and meteorites, and the reference numbers they check against are already agreed. So the missing pieces are a notebook, an agreement between two labs, and a sample somebody is willing to cut in half. If those three ever line up, the reading itself would take an afternoon.

Until then the sensible position is the one the benches have already taken: measure what turns up, write down where it came from, publish the numbers whether they come back strange or dull, and leave the question where the evidence leaves it.
